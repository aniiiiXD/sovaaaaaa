# Money: founder and website research

**Prepared:** 20 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Turn the Hyde reference into a practical starting strategy for Money, and record the website research that should guide the next design pass.

## Executive read

Hyde is a useful business-model reference because it combines specialist AI infrastructure with engineering embedded in customer work. Its public positioning is about institutions owning models trained on their own knowledge, with a training and deployment platform behind them. It is much more specific than “AI for every industry.”

Money’s current site has a strong visual direction, but it lists four industries and describes a broad capability. A visitor still cannot tell which buyer should call, what task Money will change first, or what result the buyer should expect.

**Working wedge to validate:** help Indian mid-market manufacturers and distributors make accounts-receivable work easier to act on. Bring invoice, email, ERP export, and bank context into an exception queue that explains what is late, why it is stuck, and what a finance operator can do next. Let the person approve every external message. Add cash forecasting only after the underlying records reconcile well.

This is a hypothesis informed by the existing Money concept and desk research. It is not customer-validated, and the available reports do not prove willingness to pay. The next milestone is two paid pilots, not a broad platform build.

## What Hyde actually does

Hyde describes itself as a training platform and forward-deployed model team that helps institutions use their own knowledge to build specialist models. Its Campfire platform covers infrastructure, training, evaluations, and deployment. Hyde also offers an embedded-team engagement. That combination suggests a services-to-software path: work close to a customer, solve a consequential workflow, then turn repeated implementation, evaluations, and deployment work into reusable software.

Hyde’s public case pages span financial services, retail and CPG, defense, automotive, and trading. Its homepage reports results such as a retailer improving forecast accuracy and an exchange automating regulatory workflows. These are **Hyde’s own reported outcomes**, not independently verified benchmarks. They show how Hyde tells a proof story, not what Money can promise.

The part to borrow is the operating pattern: understand the institution’s work, connect the relevant data, measure quality against examples, deploy inside the customer’s constraints, and improve with use. Money should pick a different buyer and a narrower repeatable job instead of copying Hyde’s product, language, visual identity, or customer claims.

## Market signals

### AI adoption is broad; scaled business value is less common

McKinsey’s 2025 global survey says 88% of respondents’ organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function. About one-third say their organization has begun scaling AI programs. McKinsey defines high performers as roughly 6% of respondents who report significant value and at least 5% EBIT impact. The same report finds workflow redesign is associated with higher impact. These results support selling a measurable workflow outcome, while they do not prove that a particular buyer will purchase Money.

Stanford HAI’s 2026 AI Index reports 88% organizational AI adoption in 2025, generative AI use in at least one business function at 70% of organizations, and single-digit AI-agent deployment in nearly all functions. The exact percentage depends on survey definitions; treat these as adoption indicators, not a universal census.

### India’s manufacturing MSME base is relevant, but the headline opportunity is not Money’s TAM

PwC India and ORF report that manufacturing MSMEs accounted for 35.4% of India’s manufacturing value added in FY2023–24 and nearly 48.58% of exports in FY2024–25. Their modeled estimate of USD 135.6–149.9 billion in AI-enabled value by 2035 assumes MSMEs reach 50% of national manufacturing value added. That is a scenario for economic value across the sector, not a software market size or a forecast of Money revenue.

The report also names barriers that matter to product design: affordability, skills, data readiness, trust, and vendor lock-in. The official MSME Samadhaan dashboard tracks delayed-payment applications and amounts, which is evidence that payment disputes are a recognized business problem. Neither source shows that a new AI receivables product will be bought; customer interviews and paid pilots must settle that.

## Candidate product and customer

### First customer to test

Indian manufacturing or distribution businesses with recurring B2B invoices, a small finance team, and receivables tracked across an ERP/accounting system, spreadsheets, email, and messaging. The buyer to test first is the person accountable for collections and cash visibility: a finance head, controller, or owner. This description is an initial interview filter, not a verified ICP.

### First workflow to test

1. Import invoices, customer records, payment history, and available order or delivery evidence.
2. Flag exceptions such as a missed expected payment, missing paperwork, a mismatch, or a stated dispute.
3. Show the source behind each flag and the relevant history.
4. Suggest a next step and draft a reminder for a human to approve.
5. Update a cash view only when the underlying invoice and payment status are reliable.

The first version should not make autonomous credit or collection decisions. It should help a finance operator find the right case and act with better context.

### Evidence to collect

- Time spent each week reconciling and prioritizing receivables.
- Time from a payment being missed to someone noticing and acting.
- Share of exceptions caused by missing documents, data mismatches, or disputes.
- Current cash forecast error and how the customer calculates it.
- Whether staff would trust a source-linked recommendation and what must remain human-approved.
- Who owns the budget, what system access is possible, and what the customer already pays to solve the problem.

## How Money can make money

Start with a paid workflow and data assessment. Follow it with a short, paid pilot on one team or business unit, with a baseline agreed before work begins. Convert only the repeated part into an annual product subscription, priced around the business unit, sites, or invoice volume. Charge separately for unusual integrations or deployment needs when the work is genuinely customer-specific.

That model creates early services revenue while exposing which connectors, exception logic, and evaluation tools recur across customers. Use existing models at first. Invest in custom model training only when customer data, evaluations, economics, and a measurable quality gap justify it.

Possible routes to the first customers include founder-led outreach to finance leaders, introductions from accountants and ERP implementers, and manufacturing or distribution associations. Treat each route as an experiment. Do not build a partner channel before a paid workflow has repeated.

## 90-day validation plan

**Weeks 1–2: discovery.** Interview 15 finance leaders and operators. Ask them to walk through a recent late or disputed invoice using their real process. Request a redacted sample export only after a clear data-handling agreement. Stop if the pain is infrequent, already handled well, or has no budget owner.

**Weeks 3–6: paid proof.** Secure two design partners. Baseline the current process, build a read-only exception workflow, and keep message approval with staff. Agree which metrics would justify renewal before the pilot begins.

**Weeks 7–12: repeatability.** Compare results across pilots. Keep building only the integrations and exception patterns that repeat. Continue if a customer pays, users return to the workflow, and the chosen metric improves without creating unmanageable support work.

The counts and timing above are a proposed test plan, not external benchmarks.

## Website research and design direction

The current Money homepage has an editorial, dark-green visual language and good photography. Its hero says “Intelligence that knows your world” and the industry section names financial services, manufacturing, logistics, and healthcare. The direction feels thoughtful, but the message stays broad because the page has no primary buyer, named workflow, product evidence, or proof of outcomes.

Useful reference sites:

- [Hyde](https://hyde.ai/): one clear ownership thesis, a simple description of its platform and embedded-team model, then industry-specific use cases and case evidence. Borrow the sequence from claim to mechanism to proof.
- [Glean for financial services](https://www.glean.com/solutions/industries/financial-services): translates general enterprise AI into named workflows such as underwriting, claims, client service, and compliance. Borrow its buyer-language and workflow specificity.
- [Hebbia](https://www.hebbia.com/): commits to finance, shows a visible working interface, and names tasks buyers recognize. Borrow the product specificity and restraint.

For Money, keep the subdued green palette and editorial photography, but make the hero name one buyer and one outcome. Show the receivables workflow with an authentic product view or annotated prototype. Use the industry carousel later on the page as expansion context. Replace generic benefit claims with a case study only after a real pilot produces a result that the customer approves for publication.

Suggested test headline, not final copy: **“Know which invoices are stuck, why they are late, and what your team should do next.”**
